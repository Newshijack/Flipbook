120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 1 – 4
RULE 1 Each vs. Every
'Each' focuses on individual members (used for 2 or more; acts as adjective or pronoun). 'Every' focuses on the group collectively (used for more than 2; acts only as an adjective). Both take a singular countable noun and a singular verb.
Formula: Each / Every + Singular Noun + Singular Verb | Each of + Plural Noun + Singular Verb
× Each of the participants have received their certificate.
✓ Each of the participants has received his or her certificate.
✓ Every student was present in the auditorium.
Exam Trap: 'Every of' does not exist in English because 'every' cannot be a pronoun. Use 'Each of' or 'Every one of'.
RULE 2 Both and Not Cannot Co-exist
'Both' and 'not' must never be used together to express negative meaning because of logical contradiction. Use 'Neither... nor' instead.
Formula: Do not use 'Both... not' -> Use 'Neither of' or 'Neither... nor'
× Both Rohan and Sohan did not attend the conference.
✓ Neither Rohan nor Sohan attended the conference.
Exam Trap: 'Both' is strictly positive (paired with 'and'). For two entities in negative sentences, always use 'Neither'.
RULE 3 Proximity Rule with Correlative Conjunctions
When two subjects are joined by 'Either... or', 'Neither... nor', 'Not only... but also', 'Or', or 'Nor', the finite verb agrees with the subject closest to it.
Formula: Neither S1 nor S2 + Verb (agrees with S2 in number and person)
× Neither the manager nor the employees was present.
✓ Neither the manager nor the employees were present.
✓ Neither the employees nor the manager was present.
Exam Trap: Always check the subject immediately adjacent to the auxiliary/main verb.
RULE 4 Prepositional Connectors and First Subject Priority
When two subjects are joined by 'as well as', 'along with', 'together with', 'with', 'in addition to', 'accompanied by', 'besides', 'except', 'rather than', or 'like/unlike', the verb agrees with the first subject.
Formula: Subject 1 + [as well as / along with / etc.] + Subject 2 + Verb (agrees with Subject 1)
× The Prime Minister, along with his cabinet colleagues, were welcomed.
✓ The Prime Minister, along with his cabinet colleagues, was welcomed.
Exam Trap: Do not be distracted by the plural noun immediately before the verb inside the parenthetical phrase.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 5 – 8
RULE 5 'One of the' Singular vs. Plural Verb Rules
Standard 'One of the' takes a plural noun and singular verb. However, when followed by a relative pronoun (who/which/that), the verb becomes plural because its antecedent is the plural noun. If preceded by 'the only', it takes a singular verb.
Formula: One of the + Plural Noun + Singular Verb | One of the + Plural Noun + who/that + Plural Verb | The only one of the + Plural Noun + who/that + Singular Verb
✓ One of the candidates is selected.
✓ He is one of those players who have scored a century on debut.
✓ This is the only one of his novels that is worth reading.
Exam Trap: Watch for 'the only'—it overrides the relative pronoun rule and forces a singular verb.
RULE 6 Units of Distance, Money, Time, and Weight
When a specific amount of money, distance, time, or weight is considered as a single collective unit, it takes a singular verb. If considered as divided or individual items, it takes a plural verb.
Formula: Specified Quantity/Unit as a Whole -> Singular Verb | Divided/Dispersed Units -> Plural Verb
✓ Ten thousand rupees is a reasonable fee for this course.
✓ Ten thousand rupees were distributed among the flood victims.
✓ Five miles is an easy distance to cover on foot.
Exam Trap: Look at the context: is the amount viewed as a lump sum/total distance, or are units being spent/distributed individually?
RULE 7 'A Number of' vs. 'The Number of'
'A number of' means 'many' and takes a plural verb. 'The number of' denotes a specific count/figure and takes a singular verb.
Formula: A number of + Plural Noun + Plural Verb | The number of + Plural Noun + Singular Verb
× A number of students is protesting outside.
✓ A number of students are protesting outside.
✓ The number of accidents on this highway has decreased.
Exam Trap: Remember: A = Plural (A lot), The = Singular (The exact figure).
RULE 8 'Many' vs. 'Many a / an'
'Many' is followed by a plural countable noun and a plural verb. 'Many a' or 'Many an' is followed by a singular countable noun and a singular verb.
Formula: Many + Plural Noun + Plural Verb | Many a/an + Singular Noun + Singular Verb
× Many a soldiers have died on the battlefield.
✓ Many a soldier has died on the battlefield.
✓ Many soldiers have died on the battlefield.
Exam Trap: 'Many a' means the same as 'many', but structurally requires strict singular agreement.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 9 – 12
RULE 9 Fractions, Percentages, and Proportions
With expressions like 'half of', 'two-thirds of', 'a percentage of', 'most of', or 'some of', the verb agrees with the noun in the prepositional phrase.
Formula: Fraction/Percentage of + Uncountable Noun -> Singular Verb | Fraction/Percentage of + Plural Noun -> Plural Verb
✓ Two-thirds of the book has been reviewed.
✓ Two-thirds of the books have been reviewed.
✓ Eighty percent of the crop was destroyed.
Exam Trap: The fraction itself does not determine the verb; the noun following 'of' does.
RULE 10 'More than one' vs. 'More [nouns] than one'
'More than one' is followed by a singular noun and singular verb. 'More [plural noun] than one' is followed by a plural verb.
Formula: More than one + Singular Noun + Singular Verb | More + Plural Noun + than one + Plural Verb
✓ More than one person was involved in the conspiracy.
✓ More persons than one were involved in the conspiracy.
Exam Trap: Pay close attention to where the noun sits relative to 'than one'.
RULE 11 Collective Nouns: Unanimous vs. Divided
When a collective noun (jury, committee, team, board, audience) acts unanimously as one body, use a singular verb and singular pronoun (it/its). When members act individually or disagree, use a plural verb and plural pronoun (they/their).
Formula: Unified Action -> Singular Verb & Pronoun | Divided / Individual Action -> Plural Verb & Pronoun
✓ The jury has delivered its verdict unanimously.
✓ The jury were divided in their opinions.
Exam Trap: Never mix pronouns and verbs (e.g., 'The committee has taken their decision' is incorrect).
RULE 12 Nouns Joined by 'And' Expressing a Single Idea
When two singular nouns joined by 'and' express a single unified compound concept, profession, or food item, they take a singular verb.
Formula: Noun 1 + and + Noun 2 (single idea/pair) + Singular Verb
✓ Bread and butter is wholesome food.
✓ Slow and steady wins the race.
• Note: Bread and butter are sold in this grocery store. (Two separate commodities)
Exam Trap: Common singular pairs: Rice and curry, Horse and carriage, Hammer and sickle, Crown and glory.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 13 – 16
RULE 13 Subjective vs. Objective Case After Prepositions
Any pronoun following a preposition (including 'between', 'except', 'but', 'for', 'with') must always be in the Objective Case.
Formula: Preposition + Objective Case Pronoun (me, us, him, her, them, whom)
× There is no dispute between you and I.
✓ There is no dispute between you and me.
× Everyone attended the meeting except she.
✓ Everyone attended the meeting except her.
Exam Trap: 'Between you and I' is one of the most common conversational errors in exams.
RULE 14 'Who' (Subject) vs. 'Whom' (Object)
'Who' is a subjective pronoun that performs the action (he/she/they). 'Whom' is an objective pronoun that receives the action (him/her/them). Count clauses: if finite verbs outnumber stated subjects, use 'Who'.
Formula: Who + Verb (Subject role) | Whom + Subject + Verb (Object role)
✓ The woman who called you is my manager.
✓ The woman whom you called is my manager.
Exam Trap: Do not let parenthetical clauses like 'I think' or 'they believe' mislead you into using 'whom' when the pronoun is the true verb subject.
RULE 15 Mandatory Use of Relative Pronoun 'That'
Always use 'that' instead of 'who' or 'which' after: superlative adjectives, words like 'all', 'any', 'none', 'only', 'the same', 'little', 'few', 'much', 'everything', 'nothing', and when combining a person and an animal/object.
Formula: Superlative / Indefinite Word / Person + Animal -> that
× This is the best book which I have ever read.
✓ This is the best book that I have ever read.
✓ All that glitters is not gold.
✓ The boy and his dog that crossed the road were rescued.
Exam Trap: 'Which' is incorrect after 'only', 'all', or superlative adjectives.
RULE 16 Order of Personal Pronouns: 2-3-1 vs. 1-2-3
In normal, polite, or positive contexts with multiple personal pronouns, follow the 2-3-1 rule (Second Person -> Third Person -> First Person). In negative contexts (confessing guilt, mistake, crime) or when pronouns are plural, follow the 1-2-3 rule.
Formula: Pleasant / Normal -> 2nd, 3rd, 1st (You, he, and I) | Fault / Blame / Plural -> 1st, 2nd, 3rd (I, you, and he / We, you, and they)
× I, you, and he will attend the seminar.
✓ You, he, and I will attend the seminar.
✓ I, you, and he are responsible for this financial loss.
Exam Trap: Always check whether the sentence conveys an accomplishment or an admission of guilt.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 17 – 20
RULE 17 Pronoun Case in Comparisons with 'Than' and 'As'
When comparing subjects, the pronoun following 'than' or 'as' must be in the subjective case because it represents an elliptical clause with an implied verb.
Formula: Subject 1 + Verb + [than / as... as] + Subjective Pronoun (+ implied verb)
× She is faster than me.
✓ She is faster than I (am).
✓ He respects you as much as he respects me. (Comparing objects)
Exam Trap: In colloquial speech 'than me' is common, but in formal examinations only the subjective case is accepted when comparing subjects.
RULE 18 Consistency with Indefinite Pronoun 'One'
When 'one' is used as an indefinite subject meaning 'anyone', it must be consistently followed by 'one', 'one's', or 'oneself' throughout the entire sentence, never by 'he', 'his', or 'himself'.
Formula: One -> one's (possessive) -> oneself (reflexive)
× One should do his duty diligently.
✓ One should do one's duty diligently.
× One must not boast of himself.
✓ One must not boast of oneself.
Exam Trap: Do not switch from 'one' to 'his' or 'he' mid-sentence.
RULE 19 Reflexive Pronouns with Transitive Verbs
Verbs like 'avail', 'adapt', 'adjust', 'absent', 'reconcile', 'enjoy', 'introduce', and 'exert' require a reflexive pronoun if no direct object follows. Conversely, verbs like 'keep', 'break', 'turn', 'stop', 'bathe', 'hide', 'qualify' do not take reflexive pronouns.
Formula: Transitive Verb without Direct Object + Reflexive Pronoun (myself, himself, etc.)
× He absented from the office yesterday.
✓ He absented himself from the office yesterday.
× He hid himself behind the curtain.
✓ He hid behind the curtain.
Exam Trap: 'Enjoy' takes a reflexive pronoun only when no object is mentioned (*We enjoyed ourselves at the party*, but *We enjoyed the movie*).
RULE 20 Pseudo-Subject 'It' and Subjective Pronoun Case
When 'It' acts as an introductory pseudo-subject followed by 'is' or 'was', the predicate pronoun must be in the Subjective Case, and any subsequent relative clause verb agrees with that pronoun.
Formula: It is / was + Subjective Pronoun (I, he, she, they) + who/that + Verb
× It was me who solved the puzzle.
✓ It was I who solved the puzzle.
× It is I who is responsible for the error.
✓ It is I who am responsible for the error.
Exam Trap: Always ensure the verb following 'who' agrees with the subjective pronoun preceding it.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 21 – 24
RULE 21 Parallelism with Correlative Conjunctions
Elements joined by correlative conjunctions (not only... but also, either... or, neither... nor, both... and) must belong to the exact same grammatical category (verb with verb, noun with noun, adjective with adjective).
Formula: Correlative 1 + Grammatical Form X ... Correlative 2 + Grammatical Form X
× He not only lost his wallet but also his watch.
✓ He lost not only his wallet but also his watch.
Exam Trap: Place the first conjunction immediately before the element being paralleled.
RULE 22 Inversion After Negative and Restrictive Adverbs
When a sentence begins with a negative or restrictive adverb (Hardly, Scarcely, Barely, Seldom, Rarely, Little, Never, Under no circumstances), use inverted word order (Auxiliary Verb + Subject + Main Verb).
Formula: Negative Adverb + Auxiliary Verb + Subject + Main Verb
× Seldom I have witnessed such courage.
✓ Seldom have I witnessed such courage.
× Rarely he visits his ancestral home.
✓ Rarely does he visit his ancestral home.
Exam Trap: Do not forget the helping verb (do/does/did/has/have/had).
RULE 23 Correlative Time Connectors: Hardly/Scarcely with 'When', No Sooner with 'Than'
'Hardly' and 'Scarcely' are paired with 'when' (or 'before'). 'No sooner' is paired with 'than'. Both enforce subject-auxiliary inversion.
Formula: Hardly / Scarcely + had + S + V3 ... when + Past Simple | No sooner + had/did + S + V3/V1 ... than + Past Simple
× Hardly had I entered the room than the lights went out.
✓ Hardly had I entered the room when the lights went out.
× No sooner did the train arrive when passengers rushed in.
✓ No sooner did the train arrive than passengers rushed in.
Exam Trap: Never pair 'No sooner' with 'when' or 'then', and never pair 'Hardly' with 'than'.
RULE 24 'Few' vs. 'Little' Formulations
'Few' modifies countable nouns (Few = almost none; A few = a small number; The few = all of that small number). 'Little' modifies uncountable nouns (Little = almost none; A little = a small amount; The little = all of that small amount).
Formula: Few / A few / The few + Countable Noun | Little / A little / The little + Uncountable Noun
✓ A few friends came to help me move.
✓ There is little hope of his recovery (almost no hope).
✓ He spent the little money he had on medicines.
Exam Trap: 'Few' and 'Little' without articles convey an entirely negative sense.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 25 – 28
RULE 25 Latin Adjectives Ending in '-ior' Take 'To'
Adjectives of Latin origin ending in '-ior' (senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior, anterior, posterior) along with 'prefer' and 'preferable' take the preposition 'to', never 'than'.
Formula: Senior / Junior / Superior / Inferior / Preferable + to
× He is senior than me in service.
✓ He is senior to me in service.
× Milk is more preferable than tea.
✓ Milk is preferable to tea.
Exam Trap: Never use 'more' with 'preferable' or Latin '-ior' adjectives.
RULE 26 Absolute / Non-Gradable Adjectives
Adjectives that represent absolute or extreme states (unique, perfect, universal, complete, circular, extreme, dead, impossible, round) cannot take comparative or superlative modifiers like 'more', 'most', or 'very'.
Formula: Non-gradable adjective -> No 'more' / 'most' / 'very'
× This is the most unique idea I have heard.
✓ This is a unique idea.
× The drawing was more circular.
✓ The drawing was circular.
Exam Trap: Expressions like 'more perfect' or 'most unique' are logical errors in exam questions.
RULE 27 Double Comparatives and Superlatives Prohibited
Never use two comparative degrees or two superlative degrees together in the same adjective phrase.
Formula: Avoid 'more better', 'more taller', 'most cleanest'
× He is more taller than his elder brother.
✓ He is taller than his elder brother.
× This is the most finest monument in the city.
✓ This is the finest monument in the city.
Exam Trap: If an adjective has an -er or -est form, never precede it with more or most.
RULE 28 Parallel Increase / Decrease: 'The more..., the more...'
When two related attributes or actions increase or decrease in tandem, both comparative clauses must begin with the definite article 'the'.
Formula: The + Comparative Degree ..., The + Comparative Degree ...
× Higher you climb, colder you feel.
✓ The higher you climb, the colder you feel.
✓ The more you study, the more confident you become.
Exam Trap: Make sure 'the' is present before both comparative adjectives.

RULE 29 Illogical Comparison: 'That of' and 'Those of'
Comparisons must be made between logically comparable entities. To prevent comparing an attribute of an entity to the entity itself, use 'that of' (singular) or 'those of' (plural).
Formula: Attribute of A + comparison + that of (singular) / those of (plural) + B
× The climate of Bengaluru is cooler than Chennai.
✓ The climate of Bengaluru is cooler than that of Chennai.
× The streets of Tokyo are cleaner than New York.
✓ The streets of Tokyo are cleaner than those of New York.
Exam Trap: Check whether you are comparing things of the same nature (climate to climate, streets to streets).
RULE 30 'Elder / Eldest' vs. 'Older / Oldest'
'Elder' and 'Eldest' are used exclusively for blood relatives within the immediate family and are never followed by 'than'. 'Older' and 'Oldest' apply to people outside the family, animals, and inanimate objects, and can be paired with 'than'.
Formula: Elder (blood relative, no 'than') | Older than (general age comparison)
× He is elder than his friend.
✓ He is older than his friend.
✓ Suman is my elder sister.
Exam Trap: Never say 'elder than'. If 'than' is present, the word must be 'older'.
RULE 31 First Conditional: Simple Present in Condition Clauses
In real future conditional sentences, the subordinate clause (if, when, as soon as, unless, until) must be in the Simple Present tense, while the main result clause takes future modal auxiliaries (will/can/may + V1).
Formula: If + Simple Present (V1/V1+s/es), Main Clause (will/shall/can + V1)
× If you will come tomorrow, we will discuss the project.
✓ If you come tomorrow, we will discuss the project.
Exam Trap: Never use 'will', 'shall', or 'would' inside the conditional or time clause.
RULE 32 Second Conditional: Hypothetical Subjunctive 'Were'
For unreal, imaginary, or contrary-to-fact situations in the present or future, use the Simple Past (V2) in the if-clause and 'would/could/might + V1' in the main clause. If using the verb 'to be', always use 'were' for all subjects.
Formula: If + Subject + were / V2, Subject + would / could + V1
× If I was the king, I would remove poverty.
✓ If I were the king, I would remove poverty.
Exam Trap: 'Was' is never used in unreal conditional clauses, regardless of whether the subject is singular (I, he, she, it).

RULE 33 Third Conditional: Past Unfulfilled Actions
Used for impossible past conditions that did not happen. The condition takes the Past Perfect (had + V3) and the main clause takes 'would have / could have / might have + V3'.
Formula: If + had + V3, would have / could have + V3 | Had + S + V3, would have + V3 (inverted)
× If he worked hard, he would have cleared the examination.
✓ If he had worked hard, he would have cleared the examination.
✓ Had she reached on time, she would have caught the flight.
Exam Trap: Do not mix second and third conditionals (e.g., 'If had + V3 ... would + V1').
RULE 34 Subjunctive with 'As if' and 'As though'
When expressing an imaginary or simulated condition with 'as if' or 'as though', use the past subjunctive ('were' for states, 'past perfect' for earlier unfulfilled actions).
Formula: Subject + Verb + as if / as though + Subject + were / had + V3
× He talks as if he knows everything.
✓ He talks as if he knew everything (or: as if he were an expert).
Exam Trap: Even if the introductory verb is in the present tense, the subjunctive clause requires a past form.
RULE 35 'It is high time' / 'It is about time'
When the phrase 'It is time' or 'It is high time' is followed directly by a subject, the following verb must be in the Simple Past (V2). If followed by an infinitive, use 'to + V1'.
Formula: It is high time + Subject + Simple Past (V2) | It is time + to + V1
× It is high time we start preparing for the exams.
✓ It is high time we started preparing for the exams.
✓ It is time to leave for the station.
Exam Trap: Never use the present tense or modals like 'should' immediately after 'It is high time + Subject'.
RULE 36 Gerund Following Prepositional 'To' Phrases
Phrases where 'to' is a preposition (looking forward to, accustomed to, with a view to, addicted to, habituated to, prone to, devoted to, given to, in addition to) must be followed by a Gerund (V1 + ing), not a bare verb.
Formula: Phrase with prepositional 'to' + Gerund (V1 + ing)
× I am looking forward to meet the delegates.
✓ I am looking forward to meeting the delegates.
× He went to Delhi with a view to attend the seminar.
✓ He went to Delhi with a view to attending the seminar.
Exam Trap: Do not confuse the preposition 'to' in these fixed phrases with the infinitive particle 'to'.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 37 – 40
RULE 37 'Used to + V1' vs. 'Be/Get used to + V1+ing'
'Subject + used to + V1' denotes a past discontinued habit. 'Subject + is/am/are/was/were/get + used to + V1+ing' denotes being accustomed to an activity.
Formula: Subject + used to + V1 (past habit) | Subject + [be / get] + used to + Gerund (accustomed)
✓ He used to wake up at 5 AM when he was a student.
✓ He is used to waking up at 5 AM now.
Exam Trap: Look for the auxiliary verb (be/get) before 'used to'.
RULE 38 Stative Verbs and Non-Continuous Usage
Verbs denoting states, mental perception, senses, emotions, and possession (see, hear, smell, taste, know, understand, believe, love, hate, own, belong, possess) are not used in continuous tenses in their stative sense.
Formula: Stative Verb -> Simple Present (not Present Continuous)
× I am understanding your viewpoint clearly.
✓ I understand your viewpoint clearly.
× This bungalow is belonging to an NRI.
✓ This bungalow belongs to an NRI.
Exam Trap: Continuous form is permitted only when these verbs indicate a deliberate, dynamic action (e.g., 'The chef is tasting the soup').
RULE 39 Possessive Case Before a Gerund
A noun or pronoun modifying a gerund (V1+ing acting as a noun) must be in the possessive case (my, your, his, her, their, our, Ram's), not the objective case.
Formula: Possessive Adjective / Noun + Gerund (V1 + ing)
× My parents objected to me staying out late.
✓ My parents objected to my staying out late.
× I appreciated him helping the needy.
✓ I appreciated his helping the needy.
Exam Trap: In error detection questions, replace objective pronouns (me, him, them) before an -ing noun with possessives (my, his, their).
RULE 40 Bare Infinitives with Causative and Perception Verbs
In active voice, verbs like 'make', 'let', 'bid', 'see', 'hear', 'watch', 'feel' are followed by a bare infinitive (V1 without 'to'). In passive voice, 'make' takes 'to + V1' ('let' remains bare).
Formula: Active: Subject + make/let/see + Object + V1 | Passive: Subject + be made + to + V1
× The teacher made the student to apologize.
✓ The teacher made the student apologize.
✓ The student was made to apologize by the teacher.
✓ Let him go.
Exam Trap: Never add 'to' after 'let', whether active or passive.

RULE 41 Bare Infinitives After Modal Idioms
Always use a bare infinitive (V1) after expressions like 'had better', 'would rather', 'had sooner', 'sooner than', 'rather than', and 'but/except' when preceded by a form of 'do'.
Formula: Had better / Would rather + V1 (no 'to')
× You had better to take an umbrella.
✓ You had better take an umbrella.
× He did nothing but to complain.
✓ He did nothing but complain.
Exam Trap: 'Had better' looks like a past perfect construction, but it refers to the present/future and requires a bare verb.
RULE 42 Avoiding Split Infinitives in Formal English
In formal examination standards, avoid inserting adverbs between the particle 'to' and the bare verb 'V1'.
Formula: to + V1 + Adverb (or Adverb + to + V1)
• Awkward: You need to carefully review the contract.
• Preferred: You need to review the contract carefully.
Exam Trap: Keep the infinitive unit ('to read', 'to write') intact.
RULE 43 Dangling / Unattached Participles
A participial clause at the start of a sentence must logically relate to the grammatical subject that follows. If the subject cannot perform that participial action, provide an explicit subject for the participle.
Formula: Participle Phrase, Subject (must be the doer of the participle) + Verb
× Walking in the park, a snake bit him.
✓ While he was walking in the park, a snake bit him.
× Being a sunny day, we decided to go for a swim.
✓ It being a sunny day, we decided to go for a swim.
Exam Trap: Watch out for 'Being a...' sentences without 'It' as an introductory pronoun.
RULE 44 Sequence of Past Actions: Past Perfect vs. Simple Past
When two actions happened in the past, the earlier completed action takes the Past Perfect (had + V3) and the subsequent action takes the Simple Past (V2).
Formula: Earlier Action: had + V3 ... Later Action: Simple Past (V2)
× The train left before I reached the platform.
✓ The train had left before I reached the platform.
✓ After the chief guest had delivered the speech, the ceremony ended.
Exam Trap: Do not put both past actions in the Simple Past if the time sequence is explicit.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 45 – 48
RULE 45 'Since' as a Time Conjunction
When 'since' denotes time starting from a past moment, the main clause takes the Present Perfect (or Present Perfect Continuous), while the clause introduced by 'since' takes the Simple Past (V2).
Formula: Present Perfect + since + Simple Past (V2)
× Two years passed since he left the country.
✓ Two years have passed since he left the country.
✓ I have not seen him since he graduated.
Exam Trap: Do not use Simple Past in the main clause before 'since'.
RULE 46 'Since' vs. 'For' Time Expressions
'Since' marks a specific starting point in time (since 1990, since Monday, since morning, since 4 PM). 'For' marks a duration or period of time (for three hours, for two days, for ten years).
Formula: Since + Point in Time | For + Period of Time
✓ She has been studying since 8 AM.
✓ She has been studying for three hours.
Exam Trap: Do not use 'since' with durations (e.g., 'since two years' is incorrect).
RULE 47 Sequence of Tenses with Past Principal Clauses
When the verb in the principal clause is in the past tense, subordinate clause verbs must also be in the past tense, unless the subordinate clause expresses a universal truth, scientific law, or habitual fact.
Formula: Past Main Clause -> Past Subordinate Clause (unless Universal Fact)
× He told me that he is going to Mumbai.
✓ He told me that he was going to Mumbai.
✓ The teacher explained that the sun rises in the east. (Universal Truth)
Exam Trap: Never change the tense of a universal scientific truth even if introduced by 'said' or 'proved'.
RULE 48 Simple Past Required with Specific Past Time Adverbs
Never use the Present Perfect tense with adverbs of finished past time (yesterday, ago, last week, in 1947, on Monday). Use the Simple Past (V2).
Formula: Past Time Adverbial -> Simple Past (V2), NOT Present Perfect
× I have received your parcel yesterday.
✓ I received your parcel yesterday.
× He has passed the examination two years ago.
✓ He passed the examination two years ago.
Exam Trap: The presence of 'ago' or 'yesterday' strictly forbids 'has/have + V3'.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 49 – 52
RULE 49 'Between' vs. 'Among' Distinction
'Between' is used for two entities, or for more than two when there is a mutual, reciprocal relationship or clear bilateral alliance. 'Among' is used for more than two entities considered collectively as a group.
Formula: Between: 2 entities (or reciprocal multiple) | Among: 3+ entities
✓ Divide the sweets between the two brothers.
✓ A trade treaty was signed between India, France, and Japan. (Reciprocal Pact)
✓ Distribute the blankets among the villagers.
Exam Trap: 'Between' is completely valid for more than two if mutual reciprocal relationships exist.
RULE 50 'Beside' vs. 'Besides'
'Beside' means 'by the side of' or 'adjacent to'. 'Besides' means 'in addition to' or 'apart from'.
Formula: Beside = Next to | Besides = In addition to
✓ She sat beside her mother during the ceremony.
✓ Besides Hindi, she is proficient in English and German.
Exam Trap: Check whether the sentence refers to physical location or adding information.
RULE 51 Transitive Verbs that Forbid Prepositions
Verbs like 'discuss', 'describe', 'order', 'request', 'reach', 'attack', 'resemble', 'enter', 'comprise', 'accompany', and 'marry' are directly transitive and do not take a preposition before their direct object.
Formula: Transitive Verb + Direct Object (NO preposition)
× We discussed about the budget proposal.
✓ We discussed the budget proposal.
× He ordered for two cups of tea.
✓ He ordered two cups of tea.
× The committee comprises of ten experts.
✓ The committee comprises ten experts.
Exam Trap: 'Enter into' is permitted only for signing agreements or starting discussions, not for physical entry.
RULE 52 'Die of' vs. 'Die from'
Use 'die of' when the death is caused by an internal illness, disease, hunger, thirst, or shame. Use 'die from' when the cause is external or indirect (overwork, wound, food poisoning, snake bite).
Formula: Die of + Disease / Hunger / Thirst | Die from + External Cause / Wound / Overwork
✓ He died of cancer.
✓ He died from severe blood loss following the accident.
Exam Trap: Always examine if the cause is an internal disease (of) or an external injury/factor (from).

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 53 – 56
RULE 53 Time Prepositions: 'At', 'On', and 'In'
'At' is used for exact clock times and specific points (at 5 PM, at noon, at dawn). 'On' is used for days and calendar dates (on Monday, on 15th August). 'In' is used for months, years, seasons, centuries, and broader time periods (in May, in 2026, in winter).
Formula: At + Clock Time | On + Day / Date | In + Month / Year / Period
✓ The train departs at 6:30 AM.
✓ The conference begins on Tuesday, in November.
Exam Trap: Do not use 'on' with parts of the day unless qualified by a specific day (e.g., 'in the morning', but 'on Monday morning').
RULE 54 'Lest' Requires 'Should' and Forbids 'Not'
'Lest' means 'for fear that' or 'to prevent that'. Because it carries a negative sense, never use 'not' with it, and always pair it with the modal auxiliary 'should'.
Formula: Lest + Subject + should + V1 (no 'not')
× Run fast lest you will miss the train.
× Run fast lest you should not miss the train.
✓ Run fast lest you should miss the train.
Exam Trap: Modals like 'will', 'shall', 'would', or 'may' are strictly forbidden after 'lest'.
RULE 55 'Unless' (Condition) vs. 'Until' (Time)
'Unless' is conditional ('if not'). 'Until' is temporal ('up to the point in time'). Both are inherently negative; their subordinate clauses must never contain negative words (not, never) or future modals.
Formula: Unless + Affirmative Present Clause (Condition) | Until + Affirmative Present Clause (Time)
× You will not succeed unless you do not work hard.
✓ You will not succeed unless you work hard.
× Wait here until I do not return.
✓ Wait here until I return.
Exam Trap: Identify whether the barrier is a condition to fulfill (unless) or a time moment to reach (until).
RULE 56 'Although / Even though' Paired with 'Yet' (Never 'But')
Sentences starting with 'Although' or 'Even though' may use 'yet' or a simple comma to join the independent clause. Using 'but' is a serious grammatical error.
Formula: Although / Though ... yet (or comma) [NO 'but']
× Although he worked hard, but he failed.
✓ Although he worked hard, yet he failed.
✓ Although he worked hard, he failed.
Exam Trap: Cross out 'but' whenever you see 'although' or 'though' starting the sentence.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 57 – 60
RULE 57 Strict Prohibition of Double Negatives
Never combine two negative words in the same clause. Words like 'hardly', 'scarcely', 'barely', 'seldom', 'rarely', 'forbid', 'prohibit', 'deny', 'prevent', and 'refrain' already carry negative meaning.
Formula: Negative Word + Affirmative Form (NO not / never / no)
× He denied that he had not stolen the cash.
✓ He denied that he had stolen the cash.
× She forbade me not to enter the laboratory.
✓ She forbade me to enter the laboratory.
Exam Trap: Verbs of denial and prohibition (deny, forbid, prohibit) are intrinsically negative.
RULE 58 'Doubt' / 'Doubtful' in Affirmative vs. Negative Sentences
In affirmative sentences, use 'if' or 'whether' after 'doubt' or 'doubtful' because uncertainty is expressed. In negative and interrogative sentences, use 'that' because certainty is established.
Formula: Affirmative: doubt / doubtful + if / whether | Negative / Question: doubt / doubtful + that
✓ I doubt whether / if he will pass the examination.
✓ I have no doubt that he will pass the examination.
✓ Do you have any doubt that she is sincere?
Exam Trap: Check whether the main sentence affirms doubt or negates doubt.
RULE 59 Uncountable Nouns Never Take Plural Forms
Nouns like 'scenery', 'poetry', 'furniture', 'advice', 'information', 'hair', 'luggage', 'baggage', 'machinery', 'stationery', and 'bread' are strictly uncountable. They cannot take '-s/-es' and take singular verbs.
Formula: Uncountable Noun -> Singular Verb (NO a/an, NO plural -s)
× The sceneries of the Himalayas are picturesque.
✓ The scenery of the Himalayas is picturesque.
× He gave me many advices.
✓ He gave me many pieces of advice.
Exam Trap: To pluralize their meaning, use counters: 'pieces of information', 'items of furniture'.
RULE 60 Nouns Existing Exclusively in Plural Form
Tools and clothes with two symmetrical halves (scissors, tongs, spectacles, trousers, pants, jeans, pliers, binoculars) and certain entities (premises, assets, credentials, alms) are always plural and require plural verbs.
Formula: Plural-only Noun + Plural Verb | A pair of + Plural-only Noun + Singular Verb
× My spectacles is missing from the table.
✓ My spectacles are missing from the table.
✓ A pair of spectacles was lying on the table.
Exam Trap: When 'a pair of' precedes the noun, the verb becomes singular.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 61 – 64
RULE 61 Nouns Singular in Form but Plural in Usage
Nouns like 'cattle', 'clergy', 'gentry', 'poultry', 'peasantry', 'folk', 'people', and 'police' look singular (no ending -s) but are treated as plurals and take plural verbs.
Formula: Cattle / Police / Clergy / People + Plural Verb
× The cattle is grazing peacefully in the pasture.
✓ The cattle are grazing peacefully in the pasture.
× The police has registered a case.
✓ The police have registered a case.
Exam Trap: Never add '-s' to these nouns (e.g., 'cattles' or 'peoples' in this sense are incorrect).
RULE 62 Nouns Plural in Form but Singular in Usage
Nouns ending in '-s' that name academic subjects (mathematics, physics, economics, politics), illnesses (measles, mumps, rickets), games (billiards, darts), and words like 'news' and 'innings' take singular verbs.
Formula: Subject Name / Disease / News + Singular Verb
✓ Mathematics is considered a scoring subject.
✓ This news was broadcast yesterday.
• Note: His mathematics are weak. (Plural when meaning calculations or skills)
Exam Trap: When accompanied by a possessive adjective (his, my), subject names refer to skills and take plural verbs.
RULE 63 Compound Hyphenated Modifiers Remain Singular
When a number and a noun combine to function as an adjective modifying a following noun, the modifying noun must always remain in its singular form.
Formula: Number + Hyphenated Singular Noun + Head Noun
× We stayed at a five-stars luxury resort.
✓ We stayed at a five-star luxury resort.
× He handed me a ten-rupees banknote.
✓ He handed me a ten-rupee banknote.
Exam Trap: Only the final head noun can be pluralized (*He stayed for five days at a five-star hotel*).
RULE 64 Pluralizing Compound Nouns on the Root Word
When converting compound nouns into the plural form, add the plural suffix '-s' to the primary/root noun, not to the preposition or adjunct.
Formula: Root Noun (+ s) + Preposition / Adjunct
× He has two brother-in-laws.
✓ He has two brothers-in-law.
× Many passer-bys witnessed the accident.
✓ Many passers-by witnessed the accident.
✓ Commanders-in-chief, Lookers-on, Mothers-in-law.
Exam Trap: Do not attach the plural '-s' to 'law' or 'by'.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 65 – 68
RULE 65 Apostrophe ('s) Rules: Inanimate Objects and Joint Ownership
Do not use apostrophe ('s) on non-living objects; use the preposition 'of'. In joint possession, place the apostrophe on the final noun; in separate possession, place it on each noun. Avoid double apostrophes.
Formula: Living Entity's | Leg of the table (NOT table's leg) | A and B's (joint) vs. A's and B's (separate)
× The table's surface was polished.
✓ The surface of the table was polished.
✓ Amit and Priya's joint account was opened.
× My brother's friend's sister arrived.
✓ The sister of my brother's friend arrived.
Exam Trap: Time, weight, space, and personified objects can take apostrophes (*a day's leave, nature's fury*).
RULE 66 Omission of Articles with Meals, Languages, and Institutions
Do not use 'the' before meals (breakfast, lunch, dinner), languages, or institutions (school, college, church, hospital, prison) when visited for their primary, standard purpose.
Formula: No 'the' for Primary Purpose | Use 'the' for Secondary / Specific Purpose
✓ He goes to school every morning. (As a student)
✓ His father went to the school to meet the principal. (Secondary Purpose)
✓ Dinner was served at 9 PM. (General meal)
Exam Trap: 'The French' means the French people; 'French' means the language.
RULE 67 Repetition of Articles Determines Subject Count
When two singular nouns are joined by 'and', placing an article before only the first noun indicates that one person holds both roles (singular verb). Placing an article before both nouns indicates two distinct individuals (plural verb).
Formula: The + Noun 1 + and + Noun 2 -> Singular Verb | The + Noun 1 + and + the + Noun 2 -> Plural Verb
✓ The secretary and treasurer has submitted the annual report. (One person)
✓ The secretary and the treasurer have submitted the annual report. (Two persons)
Exam Trap: Count the articles to determine whether the subject is singular or plural.
RULE 68 Formulation of Question Tags
A positive statement takes a negative tag; a negative statement takes a positive tag. Pronouns like 'hardly' and 'seldom' make a sentence negative, requiring a positive tag. 'I am' takes 'aren't I?'. Imperatives with 'Let us' take 'shall we?'.
Formula: Positive Statement -> Negative Tag | Negative Statement -> Positive Tag | I am -> aren't I?
✓ You are ready for the test, aren't you?
✓ He seldom visits us, does he?
✓ I am invited to the gathering, aren't I?
✓ Let's begin the review, shall we?
Exam Trap: 'Amn't I' is ungrammatical; always use 'aren't I?' in negative tags for 'I am'.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 69 – 72
RULE 69 Superfluous and Redundant Expressions
Eliminate redundant pairs where words repeat the same meaning: 'return back', 'revert back', 'repeat again', 'cousin brother/sister', 'consensus of opinion', 'equally as good as'. Also, 'the reason why' must be followed by 'that', not 'because'.
Formula: Return (NOT return back) | The reason why ... that (NOT because)
× The reason why he missed the train was because of the traffic jam.
✓ The reason why he missed the train was that there was a traffic jam.
× She introduced me to her cousin brother.
✓ She introduced me to her cousin.
Exam Trap: 'Because' after 'the reason why' is one of the most frequently tested errors.
RULE 70 'Too... to' vs. 'So... that'
'Too... to' expresses an excessive degree with an implied negative consequence ('more than is acceptable'). Do not use 'too' to simply mean 'very' or 'extremely' in positive contexts.
Formula: too + Adjective + to + V1 = so + Adjective + that + cannot + V1
✓ He is too exhausted to continue working.
× She is too beautiful.
✓ She is very beautiful.
Exam Trap: 'Too' carries a negative connotation unless paired in an idiomatic structure.
RULE 71 Word Order and Degree with 'Enough'
'Enough' as an adverb follows the adjective/adverb it modifies, and that adjective must be in the Positive Degree. As an adjective, 'enough' precedes the noun.
Formula: Positive Degree Adjective + enough | enough + Noun
× He is more intelligent enough to solve this puzzle.
✓ He is intelligent enough to solve this puzzle.
✓ We have enough provisions for the journey.
Exam Trap: Never place 'enough' before the adjective (*'enough smart' is wrong; say 'smart enough'*).
RULE 72 'As... as' (Equative) vs. 'So... as' (Negative Only)
'As... as' can be used in both affirmative and negative sentences to compare identical degrees. 'So... as' is strictly restricted to negative sentences.
Formula: Affirmative & Negative: as + Positive Degree + as | Negative ONLY: not so + Positive Degree + as
✓ Varun is as fast as Rohit.
✓ Varun is not as fast as Rohit.
✓ Varun is not so fast as Rohit.
× Varun is so fast as Rohit. (Affirmative is ungrammatical)
Exam Trap: Never use 'so... as' in an affirmative sentence.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 73 – 76
RULE 73 'Such... as' (Comparison/Category) vs. 'Such... that' (Result)
'Such... as' denotes category, comparison, or giving illustrative examples. 'Such... that' expresses a direct cause-and-effect relationship.
Formula: Such + Noun + as (Examples/Comparison) | Such + Noun + that (Result/Consequence)
✓ He achieved such outstanding results as were expected of him.
✓ It was such a severe storm that trees were uprooted across the city.
Exam Trap: Check whether the clause introduces examples/nature (as) or an outcome (that).
RULE 74 'Between... and' vs. 'From... to'
The correlative pair for 'between' is 'and'. The correlative pair for 'from' is 'to'. You must never mix these conjunctions.
Formula: between A and B | from A to B
× The doctor will be available between 10 AM to 1 PM.
✓ The doctor will be available between 10 AM and 1 PM.
✓ The doctor will be available from 10 AM to 1 PM.
Exam Trap: 'Between... to' is an extremely common colloquial error tested in banking exams.
RULE 75 Exact Placement of 'Only'
The adverb 'only' must immediately precede the exact word or phrase that it qualifies. Changing its position changes the grammatical subject or object being restricted.
Formula: Place 'only' directly before the restricted element
✓ Only she gave me five thousand rupees. (Nobody else gave money)
✓ She only lent me five thousand rupees. (She didn't gift it)
✓ She gave me only five thousand rupees. (No more than this amount)
Exam Trap: Look at the context of the sentence to see what is genuinely being restricted.
RULE 76 Fixed Pairs: 'Seldom or never' and 'Seldom if ever'
The standard idiomatic adverbial pairs to express rarity are 'seldom or never' and 'seldom if ever'. Never mix them as 'seldom or ever' or 'seldom if never'.
Formula: Seldom or never | Seldom if ever
× He seldom or ever neglects his duty.
✓ He seldom or never neglects his duty.
✓ He seldom if ever neglects his duty.
Exam Trap: Remember: 'or' pairs with 'never'; 'if' pairs with 'ever'.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 77 – 80
RULE 77 Fixed Pairs: 'Little or nothing' and 'Little if anything'
Analogous to Rule 76, expressions of minimal quantity are strictly paired as 'little or nothing' and 'little if anything'.
Formula: Little or nothing | Little if anything
× There is little or anything left to salvage.
✓ There is little or nothing left to salvage.
✓ There is little if anything left to salvage.
Exam Trap: Watch for mixed phrases like 'little if nothing' or 'little or anything'.
RULE 78 Inversion with Locational and Directional Adverbs
When an adverb of place or direction introduces a sentence, the verb precedes the noun subject. If the subject is a personal pronoun, normal word order is maintained.
Formula: Adverbial of Place + Verb + Noun Subject | Adverbial + Pronoun + Verb
✓ On top of the hill lived an old recluse.
✓ Out rushed the angry crowd.
✓ Here he comes. (Pronoun subject -> no inversion)
Exam Trap: Do not invert when the subject is a personal pronoun (he, she, they).
RULE 79 Placement of Adverbs of Frequency
Adverbs of indefinite frequency (always, often, rarely, seldom, never, usually) are placed before the main verb, but after auxiliary/linking verbs (is, are, was, were, have, has).
Formula: Subject + [Adverb of Frequency] + Main Verb | Subject + Auxiliary Verb + [Adverb of Frequency] + Main Verb
× He speaks always the truth.
✓ He always speaks the truth.
✓ He is always punctual.
Exam Trap: Place the adverb between the auxiliary verb and the main verb (*'He has always supported us'*).
RULE 80 Correlative Conjunctions and Parallel Parts of Speech
Correlative pairs (not only... but also, either... or, neither... nor) must connect words of the identical grammatical category. If 'but also' precedes a noun, 'not only' must precede a noun.
Formula: Verb + not only + Noun 1 + but also + Noun 2
× He not only visited London but also Paris.
✓ He visited not only London but also Paris.
× She either wants tea or coffee.
✓ She wants either tea or coffee.
Exam Trap: Identify where the second conjunction sits, and adjust the first conjunction to match.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 81 – 84
RULE 81 'As well as' Agreement and Pronoun Reference
When subjects are joined by 'as well as', verbs and possessive pronouns agree strictly with the first subject, ignoring any intervening nouns.
Formula: Subject 1 + as well as + Subject 2 + Verb (agrees with S1) + Possessive (agrees with S1)
× The principal as well as the teachers were present in their uniforms.
✓ The principal as well as the teachers was present in his uniform.
Exam Trap: Both the finite verb and the possessive pronoun must match the first subject.
RULE 82 'Though / Although' Takes 'Yet' or a Comma (Never 'But')
'Though' and 'Although' introduce concessive subordinate clauses. They can be answered by 'yet' or a comma in the main clause, but never by 'but'.
Formula: Although + Clause 1, yet / [comma] + Clause 2
× Though he is poor, but he is dignified.
✓ Though he is poor, yet he is dignified.
✓ Though he is poor, he is dignified.
Exam Trap: The conjunction 'but' creates a coordinating conflict with 'though'.
RULE 83 No 'Therefore / So' After 'Because / Since / As'
Do not combine causal conjunctions (because, since, as) with consecutive conjunctions (therefore, so, thus) in the same sentence.
Formula: Because / Since / As + Clause 1, Clause 2 (NO therefore / so)
× Since he was ill, so he did not come to class.
✓ Since he was ill, he did not come to class.
✓ He was ill, so he did not come to class.
Exam Trap: Use only one connector to show cause and effect.
RULE 84 Modals in Purpose Clauses: 'So that' / 'In order that'
When expressing purpose using 'so that' or 'in order that', use 'may' if the main clause is in the present tense, and 'might' if the main clause is in the past tense.
Formula: Present Main Clause + so that + Subject + may + V1 | Past Main Clause + so that + Subject + might + V1
✓ He studies day and night so that he may crack the examination.
✓ He studied day and night so that he might crack the examination.
Exam Trap: Do not use 'will' or 'would' in purpose clauses introduced by 'so that'.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 85 – 88
RULE 85 'Lest... should' and Negative Restriction
'Lest' inherently means 'for fear that' and must strictly be followed by 'should' (or a bare subjunctive verb). It can never be accompanied by 'not'.
Formula: Lest + Subject + should + V1 (no 'not', no 'will')
× Hold my hand lest you will fall.
× Hold my hand lest you should not fall.
✓ Hold my hand lest you should fall.
Exam Trap: Always eliminate 'not' from the clause containing 'lest'.
RULE 86 'Unless' vs. 'Until' and Subordinate Clause Constraints
'Unless' expresses a condition ('if not'). 'Until' expresses a time limit ('up to the time that'). Both are intrinsically negative words; never use 'not' or future modals (will, shall) inside their subordinate clauses.
Formula: Unless / Until + Affirmative Present Clause (NO not / will)
× Unless you do not give your best, you will not win.
✓ Unless you give your best, you will not win.
× Wait until the manager will not arrive.
✓ Wait until the manager arrives.
Exam Trap: Always remove double negatives from 'unless' or 'until' clauses.
RULE 87 'In case' vs. 'If'
'In case' indicates advance preparation for a possible future event. 'If' introduces an action that occurs only after the condition happens. Do not use 'will' or 'would' after 'in case'.
Formula: In case + Simple Present (NO will / would)
✓ Take some cash in case the card machine does not work.
× Take some cash in case the card machine will not work.
✓ Pay by cash if the card machine does not work.
Exam Trap: 'In case' is about preparedness before the event occurs.
RULE 88 Redundancy of 'Supposing if'
'Supposing' and 'if' both introduce hypothetical conditions. Using both together is redundant and ungrammatical. Use either 'supposing' or 'if', never both.
Formula: Use 'Supposing' OR 'If' (NEVER 'Supposing if')
× Supposing if he fails to submit the report on time, what next?
✓ Supposing he fails to submit the report on time, what next?
✓ If he fails to submit the report on time, what next?
Exam Trap: In sentence correction, eliminate either 'supposing' or 'if'.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 89 – 92
RULE 89 Terminal Prepositions with Infinitives Modifying Nouns
When a noun is qualified by an infinitive (to + V1), and the noun is the instrument, location, or recipient of that verbal action, the required preposition must not be omitted at the end.
Formula: Noun + to + V1 + Required Preposition
× He has no house to live.
✓ He has no house to live in.
× Please give me a paper to write.
✓ Please give me a paper to write on.
× She needs a chair to sit.
✓ She needs a chair to sit on.
Exam Trap: Test whether the preposition is needed by reversing: 'to write with a pen', 'to live in a house'.
RULE 90 'Agree with' vs. 'Agree to' vs. 'Agree on'
'Agree with' is used for a person or personal view. 'Agree to' is used for a proposal, plan, or suggestion. 'Agree on' is used for a mutual decision or topic.
Formula: Agree with + Person | Agree to + Proposal / Suggestion | Agree on + Topic / Decision
✓ I agree with you completely on this issue.
✓ The management agreed to the workers' proposal.
✓ The delegates finally agreed on the treaty terms.
Exam Trap: Never say 'agree to a person' or 'agree with a proposal'.
RULE 91 'Angry with' vs. 'Angry at'
'Angry with' is used when feeling anger toward a person. 'Angry at' is used when angry at an action, behavior, or non-personal situation.
Formula: Angry with + Person | Angry at + Action / Behavior / Thing
✓ The teacher was angry with the student for being late.
✓ The teacher was angry at the student's continuous insolence.
Exam Trap: Person = with; Behavior/Thing = at.
RULE 92 'Differ with' vs. 'Differ from'
'Differ with' means to disagree with a person in opinion. 'Differ from' means to be unlike or dissimilar in appearance, characteristics, or nature.
Formula: Differ with + Person (Opinion) | Differ from + Entity (Characteristics)
✓ I differ with you regarding this economic theory.
✓ This new software differs completely from its earlier version.
Exam Trap: 'Differ with' is about disagreement; 'Differ from' is about dissimilarity.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 93 – 96
RULE 93 'Deal in' vs. 'Deal with'
'Deal in' means to engage in trade or commerce of a specific commodity. 'Deal with' means to manage, handle, or interact with a situation, subject, or person.
Formula: Deal in + Goods / Business | Deal with + Person / Situation / Topic
✓ His firm deals in textile exports.
✓ She knows how to deal with difficult clients.
✓ This chapter deals with monetary policy.
Exam Trap: Trade = in; Handling/Management = with.
RULE 94 'Blind in' vs. 'Blind to'
'Blind in' (or 'of') refers to physical loss of sight in an eye. 'Blind to' refers metaphorically to being oblivious or willfully ignorant of faults, reality, or danger.
Formula: Blind in + Eye (Physical) | Blind to + Faults / Realities (Metaphorical)
✓ The veteran was blind in the right eye.
✓ He was blind to the shortcomings of his subordinates.
Exam Trap: Do not say 'blind of his faults'.
RULE 95 'Part from' vs. 'Part with'
'Part from' means to take leave of or separate from a person. 'Part with' means to relinquish, give away, or sell a possession, asset, or money.
Formula: Part from + Person | Part with + Possession / Money
✓ He parted from his childhood friends with a heavy heart.
✓ A miser is always reluctant to part with his money.
Exam Trap: Person = from; Belongings/Money = with.
RULE 96 'Compare with' vs. 'Compare to'
'Compare with' is used to contrast similar things to analyze points of resemblance and difference. 'Compare to' is used to draw a figurative or metaphorical comparison between fundamentally dissimilar things.
Formula: Compare with + Similar Entities | Compare to + Dissimilar Entities (Metaphor)
✓ Kalidasa is often compared with Shakespeare. (Two dramatists)
✓ The poet compared human life to a candle in the wind. (Dissimilar entities)
Exam Trap: Literal comparison = with; Figurative/poetic comparison = to.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 97 – 100
RULE 97 'Made of' vs. 'Made from'
'Made of' is used when the basic raw material undergoes no chemical change and retains its physical nature. 'Made from' is used when a chemical change takes place, transforming the original material into a new substance.
Formula: Made of + Physical State Unaltered | Made from + Chemical Transformation
✓ The table is made of solid oak.
✓ Paper is made from wood pulp.
✓ Wine is made from grapes.
Exam Trap: If the original material is still physically visible/unchanged, use 'made of'.
RULE 98 Verbs That Forbid Prepositions Before Direct Objects
Transitive verbs like 'attack', 'accompany', 'comprise', 'discuss', 'describe', 'enter', 'order', 'reach', and 'resemble' take a direct object without any preposition.
Formula: Subject + [resemble / discuss / order / etc.] + Direct Object (NO preposition)
× The army attacked on the rebel outpost.
✓ The army attacked the rebel outpost.
× The daughter resembles to her mother.
✓ The daughter resembles her mother.
× We reached at the airport on time.
✓ We reached the airport on time.
Exam Trap: Never place 'on' after 'attack', 'about' after 'discuss', or 'to' after 'resemble'.
RULE 99 'Wait for' vs. 'Await'
'Await' is a transitive verb that means 'wait for'. It takes a direct object and must never be followed by the preposition 'for'.
Formula: wait + for = await + Direct Object (NO 'for')
× We are awaiting for the final test results.
✓ We are awaiting the final test results.
✓ We are waiting for the final test results.
Exam Trap: 'Awaiting for' is a redundant error frequently tested in spot-the-error sections.
RULE 100 'Comprise' vs. 'Consist of'
In the active voice, 'comprise' means 'consist of' and takes no preposition. Never use 'comprise of' in active voice. In passive voice, 'is comprised of' is acceptable.
Formula: Active: comprise + Direct Object | Passive: is comprised of | consist + of
× The council comprises of ten distinguished scholars.
✓ The council comprises ten distinguished scholars.
✓ The council consists of ten distinguished scholars.
✓ The council is comprised of ten distinguished scholars. (Passive)
Exam Trap: Always remove 'of' from 'comprises' in active sentences.
28
120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 101 – 104
RULE 101 'Despite' vs. 'In spite of'
'Despite' is a preposition meaning 'in spite of'. It must never be followed by the preposition 'of'. 'In spite' must always be followed by 'of'.
Formula: Despite (NO 'of') | In spite + of
× Despite of his severe illness, he wrote the exam.
✓ Despite his severe illness, he wrote the exam.
✓ In spite of his severe illness, he wrote the exam.
Exam Trap: 'Despite of' is one of the most common exam traps; cross out 'of' immediately.
RULE 102 'Enter' vs. 'Enter into'
When referring to physical movement into a room, building, or place, 'enter' takes no preposition. Use 'enter into' only in figurative contexts like signing an agreement, pact, treaty, or alliance.
Formula: Enter + Physical Location (NO 'into') | Enter into + Agreement / Discussion / Treaty
× The judge entered into the courtroom.
✓ The judge entered the courtroom.
✓ India and the UK entered into a bilateral trade agreement.
Exam Trap: Physical entry = no preposition; Metaphorical partnership/agreement = 'into'.
RULE 103 Adjectives and Adverbs with Identical Forms
Certain words have identical forms for both adjectives and adverbs: 'fast', 'hard', 'late', 'early', 'straight'. Note that 'fastly' does not exist in English. 'Hard' (with effort) differs in meaning from 'hardly' (scarcely).
Formula: He is a fast runner (Adj) | He runs fast (Adv, NOT fastly)
× He drove fastly to catch the train.
✓ He drove fast to catch the train.
✓ He worked hard all his life.
✓ He hardly worked today. (Scarcely worked)
Exam Trap: Never use 'fastly'. Distinguish 'hard' (vigorous effort) from 'hardly' (scarcely/barely).
RULE 104 Position of Adverb of Manner Relative to Direct Objects
An adverb of manner must never be placed between a transitive verb and its direct object. Place it either after the object or before the verb.
Formula: Subject + Verb + Direct Object + Adverb of Manner
× He explained clearly the complex theorem.
✓ He explained the complex theorem clearly.
✓ She sang the national anthem melodiously.
Exam Trap: Never separate a transitive verb from its object with an adverb.
29
120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 105 – 108
RULE 105 Order of Multiple Adverbs: The M-P-T Rule
When adverbs of Manner (M), Place (P), and Time (T) qualify the same verb in a sentence, they must be arranged in the sequence: Manner -> Place -> Time.
Formula: Subject + Verb + [Manner] + [Place] + [Time]
× He performed yesterday brilliantly at the auditorium.
✓ He performed brilliantly (M) at the auditorium (P) yesterday (T).
Exam Trap: Remember the acronym M-P-T (Manner, Place, Time).
RULE 106 'Very' vs. 'Much' Usage Nuances
'Very' modifies adjectives and adverbs in the Positive Degree and Present Participles (V1+ing). 'Much' modifies Comparative Degree adjectives and Past Participles (V3).
Formula: Very + Positive Degree / V1+ing | Much + Comparative Degree / V3
✓ This is a very interesting novel.
✓ He was much annoyed by their reckless behavior.
✓ This version is much better than the previous one.
Exam Trap: Use 'much' with comparative adjectives (*'much stronger'*, not *'very stronger'*).
RULE 107 'Between' Cannot Directly Precede 'Each'
'Between' requires two distinct reference points. Saying 'between each' is ungrammatical. Use 'between... and', 'between [plural noun]', or 'after each'.
Formula: between + Plural Noun (NOT between each + Singular Noun)
× There should be a pause of five minutes between each round.
✓ There should be a pause of five minutes between rounds.
✓ There should be a pause of five minutes after each round.
Exam Trap: 'Between each student' is incorrect; say 'between every two students' or 'between students'.
RULE 108 Expressing Plural Quantity of Uncountable Nouns
Uncountable nouns (advice, information, furniture, bread, jewelry) cannot take an '-s'. To indicate plurality or specific quantity, precede them with count nouns like 'pieces of', 'items of', 'loaves of', or 'sheets of'.
Formula: Number / Quantifier + [pieces / items / loaves] + of + Uncountable Noun
× He ordered three furnitures for the new office.
✓ He ordered three items of furniture for the new office.
✓ He bought two loaves of bread.
Exam Trap: Do not pluralize the uncountable noun itself (*'pieces of advices' is wrong; say 'pieces of advice'*).
30
120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 109 – 112
RULE 109 Correcting Erroneous Common Collocations
Certain common colloquial phrases are grammatically incorrect in standard English: 'English teacher' strictly means a teacher of English nationality; use 'the teacher of English'. Avoid 'family members'; use 'members of the family'. Use 'pass marks' (not 'passing marks').
Formula: Teacher of English (Subject) | Members of the family | Pass marks
× He is our new English teacher. (Unless referring to nationality)
✓ He is our new teacher of English.
× All his family members were present.
✓ All the members of his family were present.
Exam Trap: 'Passing marks' is colloquial; formal examinations strictly test 'pass marks'.
RULE 110 Definite Article 'The' with Body Parts in Prepositional Phrases
When an action is performed upon a part of a person's body and the person is already identified as the object of the verb, use the definite article 'the' instead of a possessive adjective before the body part.
Formula: Verb + Object + Preposition + the + Body Part (NOT possessive adjective)
× The brick hit him on his head.
✓ The brick hit him on the head.
× She held the child by his arm.
✓ She held the child by the arm.
Exam Trap: Do not repeat the possessive pronoun (*his head, her arm*) when the person is already the verb object.
RULE 111 'Fewer' vs. 'Less' Distinctions and Exceptions
'Fewer' is used with plural countable nouns. 'Less' is used with singular uncountable nouns. Exception: When periods of time, distances, or sums of money are considered as a single collective amount, use 'less'.
Formula: Fewer + Plural Countable Noun | Less + Uncountable Noun / Unified Unit of Time & Money
× There were less participants than expected.
✓ There were fewer participants than expected.
✓ The applicant has less experience than required.
✓ The flight was completed in less than three hours. (Single unit of time)
Exam Trap: Countable items take 'fewer', but singular amounts/durations take 'less'.
RULE 112 'Elder' / 'Eldest' vs. 'Older' / 'Oldest' Constraints
'Elder' and 'eldest' are restricted exclusively to members of the same family (blood relations) and are never followed by 'than'. 'Older' and 'oldest' apply to people outside the family and inanimate objects, and are followed by 'than'.
Formula: Elder (blood relative, NO 'than') | Older than (comparative for general age)
× He is elder than his batchmate.
✓ He is older than his batchmate.
✓ She is my elder sister.
Exam Trap: 'Elder' never takes 'than'. If you see 'than', the word must be 'older'.
31
120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 113 – 116
RULE 113 The 'Other' Rule in Comparative Exclusions
When comparing an individual person or item with other members of the same class or category in the comparative degree, use 'any other' to exclude the subject from itself. Omit 'other' if comparing across different classes.
Formula: Same Class: Comparative + than any other + Singular Noun | Different Class: Comparative + than any + Singular Noun
× Iron is more useful than any metal. (Iron is itself a metal)
✓ Iron is more useful than any other metal.
✓ Diamond is harder than any metal. (Diamond is not a metal)
Exam Trap: Failing to include 'other' results in comparing an entity against itself.
RULE 114 Plural Noun and Singular Verb with Distributive Pronouns
Phrases like 'Each of', 'Either of', 'Neither of', 'One of', and 'Every one of' select one unit from a group. They are followed by a plural noun/pronoun, but strictly require a singular finite verb.
Formula: Each of / Either of / Neither of / One of + Plural Noun + Singular Verb
× Neither of the solutions are feasible.
✓ Neither of the solutions is feasible.
× Either of the candidates are qualified.
✓ Either of the candidates is qualified.
Exam Trap: The plural noun before the verb is the object of the preposition 'of', not the subject.
RULE 115 Objective Case Following 'But', 'Except', and 'Between'
When 'but' or 'except' is used as a preposition meaning 'excluding', any following pronoun must be in the Objective Case (me, us, him, her, them). Similarly, pronouns following 'between' must be objective.
Formula: Preposition (but / except / between) + Objective Pronoun (me, him, her, them)
× Everyone passed the examination except he.
✓ Everyone passed the examination except him.
× Nobody was present in the auditorium but I.
✓ Nobody was present in the auditorium but me.
Exam Trap: Do not be misled into using the subjective case at the end of the sentence.
RULE 116 Pseudo-Subject 'It' with Subjective Pronouns
When 'It' acts as an introductory pseudo-subject followed by a form of 'be' (is, was), the complement pronoun must be in the Subjective Case, and the relative clause verb agrees with that pronoun.
Formula: It is / was + Subjective Case (I, he, she, they) + who/that + Verb
× It was him who led the expedition.
✓ It was he who led the expedition.
× It is me who has drafted the proposal.
✓ It is I who have drafted the proposal.
Exam Trap: 'It is me' is common in casual conversation, but formal exams require 'It is I'.

120 RULES OF GRAMMAR RULES 117 – 120
RULE 117 Connectors with 'Such', 'Else', 'Other', and 'Otherwise'
'Else' is followed by 'but'. 'Other', 'otherwise', and 'rather' are followed by 'than'. 'Such' is followed by 'as' (category/example) or 'that' (result).
Formula: Else ... but | Other / Otherwise / Rather ... than
× It was nothing else than sheer negligence.
✓ It was nothing else but sheer negligence.
× He had no other choice but to resign.
✓ He had no other choice than to resign.
Exam Trap: 'Else' takes 'but', never 'than'. 'Other' takes 'than', never 'but'.
RULE 118 Apostrophe Rules with Plural Nouns Ending in 's'
For regular plural nouns ending in '-s', indicate possession by adding only an apostrophe at the end (do not add an additional 's'). For irregular plurals not ending in '-s', add apostrophe + s ('s).
Formula: Regular Plural ending in -s + Apostrophe (') | Irregular Plural not ending in -s + ('s)
× This is a boys's school.
✓ This is a boys' school.
✓ The teachers' common room was renovated.
✓ Children's park, Women's hostel, Men's wear.
Exam Trap: Never write 'girls's' or 'students's'.
RULE 119 Possession with Compound Indefinite Pronouns + 'Else'
When indefinite pronouns (somebody, anybody, someone, anyone, nobody, everyone) are combined with 'else', place the possessive apostrophe ('s) on 'else', not on the pronoun.
Formula: Indefinite Pronoun + else's
× This notebook is somebody's else.
✓ This notebook is somebody else's.
× Do not rely on anyone's else judgment.
✓ Do not rely on anyone else's judgment.
Exam Trap: Always put the apostrophe on 'else' (*somebody else's*, never *somebody's else*).
RULE 120 Predicative-Only Adjectives
Certain adjectives beginning with 'a-' (afraid, alike, alive, alone, ashamed, asleep, awake, aware) can only be used predicatively (after a linking verb), never attributively (directly before a noun).
Formula: Subject + Linking Verb + Predicative Adjective (NO 'an afraid man' or 'an asleep child')
× An asleep dog was lying near the doorstep.
✓ The dog was asleep near the doorstep.
× He met an afraid traveller on the road.
✓ He met a frightened traveller on the road (or: The traveller was afraid).
Exam Trap: If modifying a noun directly, replace predicative 'a-' adjectives with attributive equivalents (e.g., *sleeping child*, *frightened person*, *living creature*).
